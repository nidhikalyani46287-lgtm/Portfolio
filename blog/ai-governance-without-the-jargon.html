AI Governance Without the Jargon: A Plain-Language Field Guide

Nidhi Kalyani · Privacy & AI Governance · 7 min read
The short version
  • AI governance has a jargon problem — and jargon is how good ideas fail to reach the people who need them.
  • You don't need a technical or legal background to follow the debate. You need ten terms, in plain English.
  • This is a field guide, not a glossary: each term comes with the one sentence you'd actually say out loud to explain it.
  • If you can explain these ten to a colleague over coffee, you can read almost any AI policy headline and know what it's really about.

The single fastest way to shut people out of the AI governance conversation is to answer a plain question with an acronym. "Is this AI safe to use?" should not require a reply that contains the words conformity assessment, GPAI, and Annex III in the same breath.

I spend my days in this material — the EU AI Act, ISO standards, the NIST framework — and I've come to believe that the hardest part of AI governance isn't understanding the rules. It's translating them so a policymaker, a founder, a journalist, or a curious member of the public can act on them. Governance that no one outside the room can follow isn't governance. It's a private language.

So here's the field guide I wish I'd had when I started: the ten terms that unlock almost every AI policy headline, each with a plain-English definition and the one sentence you'd say to explain it to a friend. No prior knowledge assumed.

1. Risk-based regulation

The governing idea behind most modern AI rules — including the EU's — is that you regulate a system in proportion to the harm it could do. A spam filter and a system that screens job applicants are both "AI," but only one of them can quietly end someone's career. The law treats them differently on purpose.

Say it out loud

"The rules get stricter the more the AI could hurt someone. Low-stakes tools are barely touched; high-stakes ones carry real obligations."

2. Risk tiers (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal)

The EU AI Act sorts systems into four buckets. A handful of uses are banned outright ("unacceptable" — think social scoring by governments). A defined set is "high-risk" and heavily regulated (more on that next). Most everyday AI is "limited" or "minimal" risk, carrying light or no obligations beyond basic transparency.

Say it out loud

"Four buckets: banned, heavily regulated, lightly regulated, and left alone. The whole game is figuring out which bucket you're in."

3. High-risk system

This is the tier that generates most of the compliance work. A system is "high-risk" not because it's dangerous in the sci-fi sense, but because it operates somewhere consequential — hiring, credit, education, medical devices, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. If it lands here, the provider owes documentation, risk management, human oversight, and more.

"High-risk" is a legal category, not a personality. A boring model doing an important job qualifies; a flashy chatbot writing limericks usually doesn't.

4. GPAI (General-Purpose AI)

The large, flexible models — the ones that can write, summarise, code, and answer almost anything. Because they aren't built for a single task, they don't fit neatly into the "one system, one risk tier" model, so they get their own dedicated rules about transparency, documentation, and copyright.

Say it out loud

"The big do-everything models, like the ones behind popular chatbots. They're general-purpose, so they get a rulebook of their own."

5. Systemic risk

A subset of GPAI models are so capable and so widely used that a flaw in one could ripple across the whole economy or society. These are flagged as carrying "systemic risk" and face extra obligations — model evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting.

Say it out loud

"Some models are so big and so widely relied on that a single failure could hurt everyone at once. Those get watched more closely."

6. Conformity assessment

The formal check that a high-risk system actually meets the requirements before it goes to market — sometimes done by the company itself, sometimes by an outside body. If you've ever seen a "CE" mark on an electrical product in Europe, it's the same logic applied to AI.

Say it out loud

"It's the 'show your work before you ship' step — proving the system meets the rules, ideally before anyone's affected by it."

7. Harmonised standards

The law says what you must achieve ("appropriate risk management"); harmonised standards spell out how to do it in practice. They're the technical instruction manuals — often written by bodies like CEN-CENELEC — that turn a legal principle into a checklist an engineer can follow. Much of the real fight over AI regulation is happening here, quietly, in standards committees.

The law sets the destination. Standards draw the map. If you want to know what compliance will actually feel like, watch the standards.

8. FRIA (Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment)

Before certain organisations deploy a high-risk system, they must assess how it could affect people's fundamental rights — dignity, non-discrimination, privacy. If you know what a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is under GDPR, a FRIA is its close cousin, widened from "your data" to "your rights."

Say it out loud

"A structured 'who could this hurt, and how?' done on paper before the system goes live — not after the complaints arrive."

9. Human oversight

The requirement that a person can meaningfully understand, monitor, and — crucially — override an AI system. It's the antidote to "the computer said no." Oversight is only real if the human has the authority, the information, and the time to actually intervene.

Say it out loud

"A real person stays in the loop and can pull the plug. A rubber-stamp click at the end doesn't count."

10. AI literacy

Not a technical skill — a shared baseline of understanding, so that the people building, buying, deploying, and governing AI can talk to each other without translators. It's also, quietly, a legal requirement in the EU: organisations must ensure their staff have a sufficient grasp of the systems they work with.

Say it out loud

"Everyone touching the system knows enough to ask the right questions. This field guide is a small down-payment on exactly that."

Why this matters

AI governance only works if it reaches beyond the specialists. The regulation can be perfect and still fail if the people it's meant to protect — and the people who have to comply — can't follow the conversation. Translation isn't a nice-to-have bolted on at the end. It's part of the governance itself.

That's the part of this work I care about most: taking something dense and consequential and making it land, cleanly, for the person who needs it. Get the ten terms above, and you're no longer on the outside of the debate. You're in the room.

If you take one thing

The next time someone explains an AI rule to you in acronyms, ask them to say it the way they'd say it to a friend. If they can't, they don't understand it yet — and neither will anyone they're trying to reach.
