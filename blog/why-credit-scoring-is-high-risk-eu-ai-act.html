Why Your Credit-Scoring Model Is "High-Risk" Under the EU AI Act

Nidhi Kalyani · Privacy & AI Governance · 6 min read
The short version
  • If your model decides who gets credit, the EU AI Act almost certainly classifies it as high-risk (Annex III).
  • That triggers a full obligation set — risk management, data governance, human oversight, logging, conformity assessment.
  • It also collides with GDPR Article 22 on automated decision-making — two regimes, one model.
  • Most high-risk obligations apply from August 2026. The work to be ready starts now.

A fintech can hold billions in deposits, pass every financial audit, and still be sitting on a single unmanaged piece of AI risk: the model that decides who gets a loan.

For most of the last decade, credit-scoring models lived quietly inside risk and data-science teams. The EU AI Act drags them into the regulatory light — and a lot of fintechs haven't yet realised that the model they think of as "just analytics" is, in legal terms, a high-risk AI system.

Here's what that classification means, why it overlaps with privacy law you already know, and what to actually do about it.

The Act regulates use cases, not technology

The EU AI Act sorts AI systems into four risk tiers based on what they're used for — not how clever the model is. A simple logistic regression and a large neural network are treated identically if they're put to the same use.

▲ UNACCEPTABLE → banned (e.g. social scoring) ╱ ╲ ╱ ╲ ╱ HIGH ╲ → allowed, heavily regulated ← CREDIT SCORING IS HERE ╱ RISK ╲ ╱───────────╲ ╱ LIMITED ╲ → transparency only (e.g. chatbots) ╱────────────────╲ │ MINIMAL RISK │ → no obligations (most AI) └──────────────────┘

Annex III of the Act lists the high-risk use cases. One of them is explicit: AI systems intended to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score. (Fraud-detection systems are carved out — but that's a narrow exception, not a loophole for scoring.)

Key insight

"High-risk" is not a verdict that your model is bad. It's a statement that the stakes for the individual are high — being wrongly denied credit affects someone's housing, business, or livelihood. The obligations exist to make that decision contestable and fair.

What high-risk actually requires

Classification is the easy part. The obligations are where the work lives. For a high-risk system you need, at minimum:

  1. A risk management system that runs across the whole lifecycle, not a one-time sign-off.
  2. Data governance — training and testing data that is relevant, representative, and checked for bias.
  3. Technical documentation and logging so decisions can be reconstructed and audited.
  4. Transparency and clear instructions for the people operating the system.
  5. Human oversight — a person who can understand, intervene in, and override the output.
  6. Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity appropriate to the use.
  7. Conformity assessment and registration before the system goes to market.

The part fintechs miss: it's two regimes, not one

A credit-scoring model doesn't just sit under the AI Act. The moment it makes a decision about a person, GDPR Article 22 wakes up — the right not to be subject to a solely automated decision that produces legal or similarly significant effects.

This isn't theoretical. In the 2023 SCHUFA ruling, the Court of Justice of the EU held that generating a credit score can itself be automated decision-making under Article 22 — even when a human at the bank formally makes the final call. The two regimes layer on top of each other:

QuestionEU AI Act asksGDPR asks
FocusThe system and its risk to rightsThe personal data and the individual
Core dutyRisk mgmt, oversight, conformityLawful basis, transparency, Art. 22 safeguards
The individual getsA system that is documented and overseenMeaningful information + a route to human review

The model your data team calls "the score" is, to a regulator, both a high-risk AI system and an automated decision about a human being.

Provider or deployer? Know which hat you wear

One distinction decides how much of the obligation list lands on you. If your fintech builds the scoring model, you're a provider and carry the heaviest duties. If you buy it from a vendor and put it to use, you're a deployer — lighter, but far from nothing: you still owe human oversight, monitoring, and using the system within its intended purpose. Many fintechs are both, for different models. Mapping that per system is step one.

A practical readiness checklist

You don't need to boil the ocean before August 2026. You need to start the right way:

  • Inventory first. Build an AI use-case registry and flag everything that touches credit, scoring, or eligibility.
  • Classify each entry against the Act's tiers. Be honest about Annex III.
  • Run an impact assessment on the high-risk ones — and reuse your DPIA muscle to do it.
  • Check the Article 22 overlap: lawful basis, the information you give applicants, and a real human-review route.
  • Fix the human oversight gap — most "human in the loop" today is a rubber stamp, not genuine oversight.
  • Document as you go. If it isn't written down, to an auditor it didn't happen.
If you take one thing

Don't treat the EU AI Act as a new, separate compliance project. Treat your credit model as one system that two regimes look at from different angles — and govern it once, well.
