From Rules to Reality: 5 Takeaways from AIGG Europe 2026

Nidhi Kalyani · Privacy & AI Governance · 5 min read
The short version
  • Sovereignty has become Europe's headline priority — framed not as protectionism but as democratic agency over compute, capital, and values.
  • The conversation has moved past "what the law says" to "how do we actually implement it" — assurance, audit, and legal-engineering fluency.
  • Shadow AI and "distributed innovation" are the new governance frontier: adoption is decentralised, so governance must be too.
  • Even with high-risk timelines pushed to 2 Dec 2027, practitioners say that's not much runway — and harmonised standards are the real battleground.
  • The most durable asset isn't the regulation or the tech — it's the community of practitioners.

Last week IAPP's AI Governance Global Europe 2026 gathered in Dublin — technicians, product owners, lawyers, policymakers and regulators in one room. The signal across the sessions was clear: the law and policy are largely settled. Now comes the harder, less glamorous work of making them real.

I wasn't there, but the themes that came out of it map almost exactly onto the questions I'm working through as I move from privacy into AI governance. Here are the five that stuck with me — and why each one matters for anyone building a governance function right now.

1. Sovereignty is the new priority

With the AI Act and its Omnibus revisions now behind them, European policymakers have pivoted hard toward AI sovereignty. The framing from the keynote stage — including from the Omnibus rapporteur — was that Europe's real barriers to adoption are access to and investment in compute, capital, and energy.

Key insight

For Europe, technological sovereignty isn't a protectionist impulse — it's an assertion of democratic agency. While the US and China race to consolidate AI infrastructure and define global norms, Europe's bet is that a rights- and rules-based framework is both a competitive differentiator and a model for others.

What I found most interesting: sovereignty wasn't only discussed as a hardware problem. Several speakers reframed it around European values — the idea that technology is never neutral, and that humans should be active shapers of these systems rather than passive bystanders.

2. Implementation is upon us

The most telling shift was how quickly speakers moved past what the AI Act says and into how to do the work — procurement and contracting, copyright, auditing agentic AI systems, and integrating evaluation against technical standards.

"If harmonised standards will define what technical compliance looks like, then legal professionals need to get comfortable operating in deeply technical territory — and vice versa."

That line — from an Irish AI Advisory Council member on the Omnibus panel — captures the recurring theme: AI governance cannot be siloed. The open question everyone is wrestling with is how a general counsel and an AI engineer work fluently enough together to verify that the compliance boxes are actually checkable.

3. The cost of distributed innovation

Regulators set the legal framework; practitioners face a parallel problem. AI adoption is no longer centralised. Business units deploy tools ahead of formal governance, and employees quietly expense monthly AI subscriptions outside any procurement process — the rise of "shadow AI."

This isn't a brand-new problem — shadow IT has existed for years — but the scale and the lack of guardrails in modern AI platforms make it far riskier. The panel named the failure modes plainly:

  • Inconsistent standards across teams
  • Undocumented models
  • Liability gaps
  • Erosion of the human oversight the AI Act and basic risk management both demand
The reframe

The answer isn't to slow innovation — it's to distribute governance as fluidly as innovation has been distributed. That means a "shift-left" approach (set guardrails while the solution is being built), governance expertise spread across functions rather than trapped in one compliance team, and internal communities of practice where early lessons get shared. AI literacy is the enabler that makes all of it possible.

4. What's next for the AI Act — it's about standards

Even though EU leaders have shifted focus to sovereignty, practitioners are still deep in the AI Act's details. High-risk obligations were pushed back to 2 December 2027 — but the consensus in the room was that this is still not much time to build a real compliance strategy.

On the radarWhy it matters now
Harmonised standardsThey'll define what "technical compliance" actually looks like — and many are still under development.
Updated AI literacy requirementsLiteracy is the foundation of the distributed-governance model.
New "nudifier" rulesTargeted prohibitions added through the Omnibus.
Enhanced role of the AI OfficeA stronger central enforcement and coordination function.

The standout takeaway: more technical subject-matter experts are needed in the standards-development process. More expert input gets standards to market faster and makes them more useful. If you have technical depth, joining a standards working group is one of the highest-leverage ways to shape implementation.

5. The community is the infrastructure

The most important takeaway was also the least technical: the AI governance profession is itself a form of critical infrastructure.

The regulations will evolve. The technology will evolve even faster. What endures is the network of people who know what they've tried, what's failed, and what they wish they'd known sooner.

Europe is at a crossroads. The rules exist. Now comes the harder work of making them real — and that work is fundamentally human.

If you take one thing

Stop treating AI governance as a downstream compliance checkbox. The professionals who'll matter most are the ones who can sit between legal and engineering, distribute guardrails into the workflow, and learn out loud with the rest of the community.
