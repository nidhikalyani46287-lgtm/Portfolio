How I map to the role

An AI Governance Lead is asked to define and own an organisation's internal AI governance posture — from policy to practice — across AI, Security, Legal, and Product. Here's how each part of that mission connects to work I already do. I've been honest about where I bring proven method versus where this would be my first time owning it at company scale.

The mission
Architect the AI governance framework — standards, policies, decision structures.

Building frameworks from a blank page is what I do now. As a privacy analyst I architect privacy programs from scratch — policies, RoPAs, and decision structures — using ISO/IEC 27001 (ISMS) and ISO/IEC 27701 (PIMS) as the backbone.

ISO/IEC 27701 is my direct on-ramp to ISO/IEC 42001: the same management-system logic applied to AI — a centralised AI use-case registry, lifecycle controls across procurement/build/deploy, and EU AI Act risk-tiering translated into policy.

The mission
Drive the reduction of shadow AI and fragmentation with governance that actually gets adopted.

This is a theme I actively study and write about. My piece on AIGG Europe 2026 works through exactly this: adoption is decentralised, so governance has to be distributed as fluidly as the innovation — shift-left guardrails, communities of practice, and AI literacy — rather than a compliance team saying no.

My instinct is always operational: controls people can act on, not documentation that sits unread.

The mission
Evaluate and select AI platforms with attention to security, compliance, and long-term fit.

My Article 28 processor assessments already extend to GenAI tools, Copilot extensions, and LLM-embedded SaaS — evaluating training-data use, data lineage and residency, prompt-injection exposure, and model drift before a tool enters the environment.

Hands-on with OneTrust, Vanta, and Securiti for the vendor-risk and monitoring workflow. The question I ask of every tool is the same: what happens to the data, and who is accountable.

The mission
Lead cross-functional alignment between AI, Security, Legal, and business stakeholders.

I sit at that intersection by training. A lawyer who drafted SaaS/vendor contracts and advised banks on regulatory compliance, now fluent in the security and data-governance side (ISO 27001, DPIAs, incident response) — and technical enough to open the browser and point to the violation, not just cite it.

Translating complex trade-offs into clear, defensible decisions is the core of legal advisory work.

Technical breadth
Enough credibility to evaluate AI tooling and ask the right hard questions.

I make the technical layer concrete. My GDPR & DevTools carousel walks through finding real violations in the browser's Network tab — pre-consent trackers, US data transfers, IP leakage. It's a small proof that I bridge the legal text and the actual request/response.

On AI specifically, I frame risk through the EU AI Act tiers, the fundamental-rights impact assessment, and the NIST AI RMF Govern–Map–Measure–Manage cycle.

Honest note
Execution from 0 to 1, at company scope.

Straight answer: I've built privacy frameworks from zero for client organisations, but I haven't yet owned a company-wide AI governance function end-to-end — that would be the next step. What I bring is the method, the standards fluency, and a bias for practical adoption; what I'd be growing into is the executive-scope ownership. I'd rather be clear about that than oversell it.
